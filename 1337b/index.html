1337b What education is for.

Across one Bekker page, Aristotle tests education by its effects and its ends: what is necessary, what is liberal, why music belongs in the curriculum, and why leisure—not amusement—is the end of occupation.

“…the first principle of all action is leisure.”

— Benjamin Jowett, Politics VIII.3

Condensed rendering of 1337b30–42

The earlier question about music turns on leisure. Nature asks that we learn not only how to conduct necessary activity rightly, but how to use leisure nobly. If occupation and leisure are both necessary, leisure is more choiceworthy and is the end. We must therefore ask what we ought to do when at leisure.

Surely not merely play, for then play would have to be the end of life. Play instead belongs amid our occupations: the person who labors needs rest, and play exists for relaxation. Because occupation comes with exertion and strain, play should be introduced at the fitting time, as one administers a medicine. The final sentence continues at 1338a1.

01

Leisure is an end

Work points beyond itself. Aristotle asks politics and education to account for the life made possible when necessity loosens its grip.

02

Play is medicine

Amusement restores a tired person. It matters, but it is restorative rather than the final purpose of a free life.

03

Purpose matters

Even liberal study needs limits. Whether an act is liberal or servile depends partly on the end for which it is done.

The word remembers

“School” carries scholē inside it.

English school passes through Latin schola from Greek scholē: leisure, discussion, lecture, school. The history of the word preserves the argument of the passage. Learning belongs to the space opened when immediate necessity no longer consumes the whole day.

That does not make education idle. It gives education a larger job: forming judgment, attention, taste, and the capacity to spend freedom on things worth doing for their own sake.
